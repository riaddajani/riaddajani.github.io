Extreme Theory: Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks

A rigorous theoretical framework applying extreme theory to Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks, extending the foundational KA representation theorem to neural architectures.

Co-authored with Misha Coen · unpublished

Download PDF →

What it is

This paper applies Smolensky's extreme theory methodology to Kolmogorov-Arnold Networks (KANs), recently introduced by Liu et al. (2024). By systematically building from the axiomatic foundations of the Kolmogorov-Arnold representation theorem, we derive theoretical laws and empirical predictions about KAN behavior, interpretability, and performance characteristics.

Unlike conventional Multi-Layer Perceptrons (MLPs) that use fixed activation functions, KANs employ learnable activation functions on network edges—replacing linear weight matrices with parameterized univariate functions (typically splines). This architectural shift enhances model interpretability, compactness, and accuracy while providing a more natural alignment with the mathematical structure of the KA theorem.

The Kolmogorov-Arnold Representation Theorem

The KA theorem states that any continuous multivariate function \(f : [0,1]^n \to \mathbb{R}\) can be decomposed as:

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = f(x_1, \ldots, x_n) = \sum_{q=1}^{2n+1} \Phi_q \left( \sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p}(x_p) \right) \]

where \(\Phi_q\) and \(\phi_{q,p}\) are univariate continuous functions. This remarkable result shows that any multivariate function can be represented using only:

  • Univariate functions: \(\phi_{q,p} : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) (inner transformations)
  • Addition: The only "true" multivariate operation
  • Outer functions: \(\Phi_q : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) (final transformations)
  • Finite superposition: At most \(2n + 1\) auxiliary functions for \(n\)-dimensional input

Why it matters

While mathematically elegant, the KA theorem was long considered impractical for neural network applications due to computational complexity and the difficulty of learning appropriate univariate functions.

This work bridges that gap by:

  • Establishing a formal deductive framework connecting the KA theorem to practical neural architectures
  • Deriving fundamental laws governing KAN behavior from first principles
  • Making testable predictions about generalization, computational efficiency, and robustness
  • Providing theoretical grounding for why KANs exhibit superior interpretability compared to MLPs

Key contributions

  • 16-step extreme theory derivation: From fundamental dimensions through axioms, laws, and theorems to empirical predictions
  • Three core theorems: Universality of function representation, optimization of model complexity, and stability/predictability in applications
  • Formal parameter analysis: Systematic identification of network parameters (spline types, depth, width) and their observable properties
  • Empirical validation framework: Alignment of theoretical predictions with reported results from Liu et al. (2024)

Fundamental axioms & derivative laws

The paper establishes axiomatic foundations and derives key laws governing KAN behavior:

Axiom: Decomposability

For all continuous \(f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}\) on a compact set, there exist univariate functions \(\phi_{q,p}, \Phi_q : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}\) satisfying the KA representation.

Law of Partial Derivatives

Given continuous, differentiable univariate functions, the partial derivative of \(f\) with respect to input \(x_p\) follows from the chain rule:

\[ \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_p} = \sum_{q=1}^{2n+1} \Phi_q' \left( \sum_{p'=1}^{n} \phi_{q,p'}(x_{p'}) \right) \cdot \phi_{q,p}'(x_p) \]

This derivative structure reveals how KANs naturally capture input sensitivity through learnable univariate function gradients.

Law of Composition and Superposition

The KA structure enables recursive layering—each layer's output becomes input for the next:

\[ f(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{q=1}^{2m+1} \Psi_q \left( \sum_{r=1}^{m} \theta_{q,r} \left( \sum_{p=1}^{n} \phi_{r,p}(x_p) \right) \right) \]

This directly motivates deep KAN architectures by stacking multiple KA-structured layers.

Three core theorems

Theorem 1: Universality of Function Representation

Derived from axioms of Universality and Decomposability

Every continuous multivariate function on a compact domain can be universally represented by a finite composition of univariate functions and additions.

Theorem 2: Optimization of Model Complexity

Derived from axioms of Finite Complexity and Minimal Representation

The representation of any continuous multivariate function by exactly \(2n + 1\) univariate functions optimizes computational efficiency and model complexity. This provides a theoretical lower bound on KAN width.

Theorem 3: Stability and Predictability in Applications

Derived from axioms of Continuous Transformation and Smooth Integration

Functions structured under the Kolmogorov-Arnold framework respond continuously to input variations, ensuring stable and predictable behavior in applications. This explains KAN robustness to noise observed empirically.

Technical highlights

The paper systematically works through:

  • Fundamental dimensions of the KA theorem (multivariate decomposition, boundedness, finite superposition)
  • Central theoretical constructs (representation, superposition principle, finite composition, continuity)
  • Axiomatic foundations stated over continuous transformations and decomposability
  • Derivative laws for partial derivatives, composition/superposition, and uniform convergence
  • Formal deduction of observable properties from system parameters
  • Mapping from the idealized KAN system to empirical implementations

Context

This work represents a continuing and developing understanding of the KA theorem and KANs—not just for the authors but for the field as a whole. The paper demonstrates how classical mathematical theorems can be revitalized through modern neural network architectures when approached with rigorous theoretical frameworks.
